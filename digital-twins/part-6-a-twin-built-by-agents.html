A twin built by agents: and why a second city took days

The Newcastle twin was built in 64 working sessions with AI coding agents, and no session remembered the one before it. This post is the technology that made that work - the repository kept as the agents' memory and read like a table of contents, and a ladder that turns every mistake into a check - and the proof that it transfers: a second city, Mumbai, stood up on the same framework in days.

October 2026 · ~13 min read · Part 6 of a series

Sixty-four new engineers

Between 10 August and 30 September the twin took 709 commits, 94 pull requests and 64 sessions with AI coding agents - Claude Code, with the same skills later ported to OpenAI's Codex. What Part 5 described - a population, a network, a ledger, a scoreboard - was written by agents, reviewed and steered by me. The simulation is not the hard part of that arrangement. Memory is. Every session starts with none: whatever the last one understood, decided or half-finished is gone unless it was written down somewhere the next one will look.

I had met this problem from the other side. The software engineering agent I wrote about last year onboarded a repository the way a new engineer would: summarise every file, then every package, and use the hierarchy like a table of contents. This project inverts that. Instead of the agent building a table of contents from a repository, the repository is kept as one, so that any agent with no memory can read its way to the current state in a fixed budget.

The first lesson arrived on day four. The context for picking the project up had been living in a prompt pasted into each new chat. On 14 August it moved into the repository, and the commit says why: "Previous sessions kept this in a prompt, which is how it drifted from the repo it describes."

The repository is the memory

A new session reads, in order, about 600 lines and no more:

  • The goal - one page saying what the twin is for and the ten hard requirements it answers to.
  • The board - one page: the twelve-mode scoreboard, what runs, what is next. Most of it is generated from the run records, not written.
  • The brief - facts that expire, each paired with the command that re-derives it, then the traps that have already cost a day each.
  • One position page - the current truth on the topic the next task touches, at most 130 lines, every figure with its source.
  • One section of the record - the dated, append-only record of every decision, found through its index. The record is over 18,000 lines. Nobody reads it whole.

The rule that holds this together is that a fact has one home. The number of runs, the count of registry fields, the current scoreboard: each lives in one artefact, and every document that states it is either generated from that artefact or pinned to it by a check that fails when they disagree. Prose is either a dated record that is never edited, or a current page that is rewritten in place. It is never appended to.

That rule was learned the hard way. By 30 August the record was 911 KB and out of order, the "one-page" board ran to 805 lines under a heading that said it was not a diary, the brief named five different families of runs as current, and orienting a new session took about forty minutes. The restructure that day produced the funnel above. The checks that keep it that way cap each page's size, require every figure to carry a source, and refuse to open a pull request while any document is out of step with its artefacts.

Onboard, work, hand off

Two skills bracket every session. Onboard reads the funnel, re-derives every expiring fact by command, runs the full gate, and reports the state in forty lines before touching anything. Handoff does the reverse: it rewrites the position pages the session touched, appends one section to the record, regenerates the board, rewrites the brief from its template, triages open issues on evidence, runs the gate, and lands everything as one pull request that it watches until it merges. Both follow a single written contract, created after the two skills' own copies of "what to check" had drifted apart.

The clearest evidence that the bracket matters came from the sessions that skipped it. Around 19 September, a run of work arrived with no handoff at all: twenty-two record sections and 131 uncommitted files. When the next session checked those changes against the Newcastle model, three of them were wrong. Nothing about the work itself was careless. It simply had not passed through the step whose job is to check it against everything else.

A rule a session can skip is a wish

That phrase is a comment in one of the project's hooks, and it is the whole method. A mistake is first written down as a trap in the brief, or as a note in the agent's memory. If it happens again, it becomes a step in a skill. If it happens again after that, it becomes a check in the gate, or a hook that refuses the action outright. Written advice is the bottom rung, not the top.

The ladder also has to resist being gamed, including by the agents. An early version of the rule "no run while an issue is open" only checked a label. A measured defect was then deliberately left unfiled, because filing it would have turned the launcher red. The gate's own documentation now says it plainly: "A gate that makes filing a defect costly is selecting against being told." It asks instead for the specific measurement the run will supply, and reports when that measurement is due.

Where the human stays in the loop

Agents do most of the work; three things stay with me, and each is structured so that it costs little attention.

  • Decisions are asked once. Every open decision lives in one ledger with its options, the recommended one first. A session puts it to me as clickable choices at the end of onboarding, records the answer, and never asks it again. This replaced a board where the same four decisions had sat for several sessions, re-recommended by six reports.
  • Compute is approved at a stated price, and each approval is spent on use. A full run costs a day of the workstation, so none starts without my approval of its stated cost. The price comes from the project's own measured runs rather than an estimate, after one run priced at 260 seconds an iteration ran at 376.
  • The project audits itself, on a schedule it cannot inflate. A project-report skill sends six agents, one per area and each with a research budget, to assess the whole repository and place it against comparable twins. Its recommendations go into a ledger - 84 so far, 58 taken, each tied to the commit that took it. Its eighth run found a flaw in its own cadence: nine reports in eight days had had 76 of 105 recommendations taken while the number of modes inside 10% had not moved. It now runs once per new reading, not once per session.

The proof: a second city

The test of whether all this is a framework or one city's scaffolding is whether a second city fits. That was prepared early. On 14 August the code had 338 file paths across 46 scripts that assumed Newcastle was the only city. They were moved behind a single module that knows where a city lives, and the rule became that the framework may not name a place. A city is now one folder that has to supply a described contract.

On 19 September the priority changed: a running, broad, all-mode simulator of Mumbai came before more precision in Newcastle. Within about four days the Mumbai metropolitan region had its own folder and its own pipeline:

  • 27 million synthetic residents in 6 million households, drawn from the 2011 census and carried forward to 2026.
  • A road network of 846,699 links from OpenStreetMap, with gradients from a global elevation model.
  • Suburban trains from the printed timetables of the Western and Central Railways: 3,289 trains against the 3,234 the operators publish. The metro runs at its published headways, seven commuter ferry crossings run too, and the metro's daily ridership from the government's open-data portal became its target.
  • 572 of 600 catalogued sources acquired, each with a stated use, hashed into a 1,459-file manifest.

Acquiring it was its own lesson. Many Indian government hosts refuse connections from an Australian address, so sources came through Internet Archive copies, a VPN endpoint in India, and, for data behind a captcha, downloads from my own logged-in browser that a script then imported with their provenance. The one approach the agents were not allowed to take was weakening TLS to get past a refusal.

Mumbai does not have a ridership reading yet, and the reason is instructive. MATSim scales every road's capacity with the sample, so at a 1% sample each link passes one vehicle every 200 seconds, and on a network whose median link is 65 metres the city gridlocks regardless of how much memory the machine has. The smallest honest sample is about 10%: 2.7 million agents and roughly 247 GB of working memory, several times what this workstation has. Mumbai runs through the same harness, checks and scoreboard as Newcastle, with its differences declared as switches rather than written as code. The machine is what it now waits on.

What transfers

Treat every agent session as a new engineer with no memory, and keep the repository as the table of contents it reads, with a budget. Give every fact one home and generate the rest. Let mistakes climb from advice to enforcement, because a rule a session can skip is a wish. Keep the human on the decisions and the spending, asked once and priced from evidence. None of it is specific to transport - and the second city is the proof that none of it was specific to Newcastle either.

Mumbai is also where the next chapter came from. A twin that can stand up a city of 27 million people on one contract caught the attention of a department of the Indian government, and the proposal we prepared for it is the subject of the next post.
