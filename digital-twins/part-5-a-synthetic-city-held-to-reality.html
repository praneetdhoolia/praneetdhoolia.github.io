City digital twin · Part 5 · The synthetic city

A synthetic city on one engine: built, then held to reality

Part 4 ended with an upgrade path: real demand, real targets, many runs, and a ledger of where every number came from. A second build had been running alongside it since 10 August. This is what that build became - a census-drawn population choosing its own trips on one engine, with every input on a ledger - and what happened when it was held to twelve real ridership figures.

October 2026 · ~14 min read · Part 5 of a series

The build that ran alongside Part 4

Part 4 was a weekend build: one precinct, one question, demand I wrote down myself, thirteen runs. It closed by listing what would make its numbers defensible - the official mode-split targets as inputs, real station capacities, real traffic counts to calibrate against, and a sweep of seeds - and by saying that the valuable object was never the map but "the precomputed grid with its provenance attached".

That list was not hypothetical. Since 10 August a second, much larger build had been running in parallel, on a city chosen for a question rather than an event. Newcastle, in New South Wales, opened a 2.7 km light rail in 2019. The state's Auditor-General found in 2018 that its business case had been written after the decision to build it, and no ex-post evaluation exists. The first design was a counterfactual study: simulate Newcastle with the tram and without it, and compare - Part 1's sixth correction taken literally.

On 24 August the goal changed. Answering one question about one tram needed a model of the whole city's travel that could be trusted first, so the twin itself became the goal: twelve ways of travelling, each moving physically through the real network, each checked against its real-life ridership. The light rail became the first question the finished twin will be pointed at, not the reason it exists.

People are generated, not authored

In Part 4 the crowd was a number: 63,000 people, 45% by car, 2.5 to a car. Everything downstream was arithmetic on my assumptions. The Newcastle twin starts somewhere else. It draws households and people for each of 1,500 census areas (SA1s) so that, added up, they reproduce what the census published there: household sizes, dwellings, cars per household, age and sex, work status, schooling and income.

This is Part 3's census exercise grown up. There, a published SA1 count was split down onto land parcels. Here, the same published counts are used to generate the people themselves, and the rule from Part 3 still holds: the synthetic population must sum back to what was published, and nothing finer is claimed as observed.

Then each person gets a day. Home-anchored tours - to work, to school, to the shops, dropping a child off - are generated to match the state's Household Travel Survey: 3.47 trips per person per day. Destinations sit on real buildings and points of interest. Driver licences come from the transport department's published counts for each local government area and age band, not from a guess. Households own the cars the census says they own, so two drivers with one car have to share it.

One thing is deliberately not generated: how anyone travels. Nobody is assigned a mode. That is left to the simulation, which is the point of Part 1's third correction: people are not particles, so the model has to let them choose.

One engine, and everything moves

The engine is MATSim. Where SUMO moves vehicles whose routes you give it, MATSim moves people who each carry a plan for the day, and it iterates. Every simulated person executes their plan on the network, where the plans collide in queues. Each plan is then scored: time spent at activities is good, time spent travelling, waiting and walking to a stop is bad, fares and parking cost money. Between iterations a share of people try something different - a new route, a different departure time, another mode - and over a few hundred iterations the population settles into a state where few can do better. The mode shares come out of that settling. Nobody types them in.

The first design used two simulators: MATSim for the city, SUMO for a signal-accurate model of the tram corridor, joined by an outer loop. On 25 August SUMO was removed by recorded decision. The record is blunt about its corridor model: "built six times, simulated zero times". Every question it had been kept for turned out to have a native MATSim form - level crossings as timed road closures, the tram's charging dwell from its timetable, a lost traffic lane as a network patch. Its one unique offer, thirty seeded replications for travel-time variance, never fitted on one workstation. Part 4 chose the mesoscopic engine for the same reason: speed buys runs, and runs buy comparisons.

One engine made a stricter rule affordable: nothing teleports. Walkers, cyclists, motorcyclists, cars, taxis, buses, trains, trams, ferries and trucks all move through the same network with their own speeds and road space, and a car can overtake a walker. A passenger getting a lift boards the driver's actual car, and the driver detours to pick them up. Where a physical model would have to invent something, the twin declines. Coal trains are the example: putting them on the passenger model's tracks would "fabricate an interaction", so trains appear as what the road network actually feels - the boom gates at two level crossings closing 405 times on a weekday, for passenger and coal trains together.

The traffic signals are the clearest case of deriving instead of assuming. Sydney's adaptive signal system, SCATS, has published algorithms, but Transport for NSW would not release the operated phase plans and offsets. The first design's fallback was to sweep a cycle time. The twin instead implements the published algorithm: each intersection measures how saturated its approaches were, stretches or shortens its cycle towards a target, and re-splits green time. What stays unobtained - the operated offsets - is written down as unobtained, not invented.

The ledger is the product

Part 4 put its provenance in a table under the map. The twin makes it structural. Every value a run can be sensitive to - 600 of them, from walking speed to the price of parking - is declared in a registry, never typed into a script. Each one says where it came from, and each one that was assumed, taken from the literature, measured or derived carries a range to sweep it over, a stated rule for holding it fixed, or the identity it is derived from. A check counts any number a script decides for itself; it stands at zero, and a change that adds one is not finished.

The files get the same treatment. All 968 derived and downloaded files are listed in a manifest with a hash, a row count, the script that produced them, the source, the retrieval date and the licence - 733 under CC-BY and 220 under the OpenStreetMap licence, whose share-alike terms follow anything built from them. The manifest is checked in CI, so a file that changes without its entry changing fails the build.

The rule that grew out of this is stronger than "label your assumptions": what can be derived is never assumed. A sweep is the fallback only where derivation is genuinely impossible, and the reason has to be stated. It sounds pedantic until it pays. Early on, a study area typed into a script as a rectangle quietly clipped 87 of the 1,500 census areas out of the road network, and nobody noticed for three phases. Now no coordinate may appear in code: the extent is derived from the council boundaries, and a check fails the build if anyone types one in. The check's own docstring puts the philosophy best: "A gate you can satisfy by editing prose is not a gate."

Held to twelve real numbers

Part 4 had sanity checks and no targets. The twin has twelve: car driver, car passenger, walk, bike, motorbike, taxi, bus, heavy rail, light rail, ferry, truck and freight train. Each is scored on the basis its real figure is published on. The travel survey gives residents' shares of trips. Opal card data gives weekday boardings at the disclosed stations, on the light rail and at the ferry wharves. Trucks are scored against classified road counts, and freight trains against the timetable and a published survey of coal movements. There is never an umbrella "public transport" row: a model that gets public transport right by overshooting trains and undershooting trams is wrong twice.

Three rules keep the scoring honest:

  • A gate. Every 100 iterations the run reads all twelve modes. If any mode is 20% or more off its target, the standing rule is to stop and look for the cause at the root - missing data, a bias, a mechanism that is not there - rather than paper over it with a constant. (A control run that exists to be compared with a treatment runs on to its horizon, and is read there.)
  • A holdout. Of 210 validation targets, 143 are held back. The calibration code is structurally unable to read them, and they are opened once, at the end.
  • Bands, not diffs. Part 1's fourth correction said one run is not the answer. It came back in an unexpected form: three MATSim runs of the same build, same seed, diverge by the second iteration, because a counter inside MATSim's random numbers races across twenty threads and a handful of people get different routes. The mode shares came out the same, but no difference between two runs can be read as exact. The transit mapper is worse: rebuilt from the same inputs, it lays nearly one route in five on different streets. So every run belongs to a declared comparability family, nothing is compared across a family, a sample size or a network build, and a difference is read as a band.

What the scoreboard says

Two of twelve is not a calibrated twin, and I would rather say so than tune it into looking like one. Every mode past the bar has a named mechanism behind it, and none of them is a constant waiting to be nudged. People drive 59% of trips shorter than a kilometre, because the model does not yet charge the walk to and from a parked car. Almost half of public-transport routing requests find no transit route at all. And a person without a car, on a trip no driver is bound to and no transit serves, is left with walking, cycling or a taxi - which is where bike's and taxi's overshoot sits. Heavy rail is more than double its real boardings and the light rail is three-quarters short. The mode this whole project started from is, for now, the furthest from the truth.

The discipline that keeps this honest is the one that is hardest to hold: the mode constants stay as priors. Every choice model has a constant per mode that can absorb almost any error, and solving those constants against the scoreboard would pull most rows towards their targets within a few runs. It would also hide every missing mechanism behind a number. So the twin changes what people can do, not how much it likes each mode, and it accepts a red scoreboard while the mechanisms are built.

Built is not calibrated

Nothing in this post is a forecast, and no parameter has yet been fitted to the targets. What exists is a machine that can be checked: every input traced, every mode scored on its own basis, every run recorded with the reason it ended. Part 4's lesson still holds - pick the metric before the policy - and the twin now has twelve metrics that nobody gets to pick after the fact.

What a run costs

A run simulates a quarter of the population, sampled by household so that families travel together: about 158,000 people on a network of 368,000 links, with every bus, train, tram and ferry on its real timetable. Two hundred and fifty iterations take about 28 hours on one workstation with 64 GB of memory. There are 250 run directories on disk. More than half are short probes that price a change before anyone commits a day to it, and only a handful are results in the strict sense: full runs that reached their last iteration.

The workstation turned out to be the weakest part. By late September, 41% of the machine-hours spent on runs had gone to runs that died - to a forced Windows Update restart at iteration 237 of 250, to a disk controller crash, to a shutdown from the Start menu. None of that is simulation code, and neither is most of what now prevents it. Keeping a two-month build honest with that little human time is its own piece of technology, and it is the subject of the next post.
